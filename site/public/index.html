A field guide / from possibility to progress

Better ideas begin before the brainstorm.

Notice what could be better. Decide what is worth pursuing. Then generate ideas aimed at a clear outcome.

01 / Observe

Look for the space between what is and what could be.

An opportunity is an unmet need, a recurring friction, or a new possibility. Start with evidence from real life before deciding what the solution should be.

01 / Friction

Follow the workarounds

Watch where people repeat steps, invent shortcuts, wait, give up, or ask for help. A workaround often points to a need the current process misses.

What do people keep doing despite the inconvenience?
02 / Change

Notice what has shifted

New tools, constraints, expectations, and habits make previously impossible or unimportant ideas newly useful.

What can people do now that they could not do a year ago?
03 / Perspective

Change the viewpoint

Ask who benefits from the current arrangement and who struggles with it. Look at the first use, the handoff, and the edge cases.

Whose experience have we not examined?
Capture, then frame

Write observations as needs or tensions: “New team members cannot tell which decision is current,” rather than “We need a new dashboard.” The first leaves room for better solutions.

02 / Decide

Choose a goal that gives creativity a direction.

Not every opportunity deserves immediate attention. Select one by weighing its importance, the evidence behind it, and your ability to make progress.

A / Value

Does it matter?

Who would benefit, and how much would their experience improve?

B / Evidence

Is it real?

What have you observed? What assumption still needs testing?

C / Agency

Can you act?

What can you change with the time, access, and resources available?

D / Learning

Will you learn?

Can a small experiment reveal whether this is worth a larger effort?

Frame the goal
Help [specific people] achieve [valuable outcome] in [context], while respecting [key constraint].
03 / Create

Generate options for the goal you chose.

A clear goal makes divergent thinking more useful. Produce genuinely different approaches first; judge them against the goal afterward.

Reframe

Ask “How might we…?”

Turn a fixed problem into an open question. Make it narrow enough to guide ideas and wide enough to allow different answers.

Try: How might we make the next step obvious without adding more meetings?
Expand

Vary the mechanism

Generate options through different means: remove a step, automate one, change the timing, shift responsibility, or make information visible.

Try: Produce five ideas that work in five different ways.
Challenge

Use a useful constraint

Temporarily limit time, money, technology, or complexity. Constraints can uncover simple ideas that an unrestricted brainstorm overlooks.

Try: What could work with no new software?
Test

Make the idea falsifiable

Choose promising ideas by their fit with the goal, then find the smallest test that would show whether each one helps.

Try: What would you need to see to change your mind?
A worked example

From a vague complaint to a testable idea.

Imagine a team that keeps losing track of decisions made in meetings. “We need better meetings” is too broad to direct creative work.

01 / Opportunity

Spot the pattern

People repeatedly ask which decision is final and where its rationale lives. New colleagues struggle most.

02 / Goal

Name the outcome

Help team members find the current decision and its owner in under two minutes, without adding another meeting.

03 / Idea + test

Try a small change

Add a one paragraph decision record to the existing project page. Ask three colleagues to find a decision a week later.

Put it to work / 15 minutes

Take one real challenge through the sequence.

Use a notebook or work with a partner. Keep the observation, goal, and ideas separate until you have explored each one.

  1. What recurring friction or overlooked possibility have you observed? Describe one concrete instance.
  2. Who is affected, and what would a meaningful improvement look like for them?
  3. Which opportunity scores best on value, evidence, agency, and learning?
  4. Write one “How might we…?” question tied to that goal.
  5. Generate at least six different approaches. Pick one small test and decide what evidence would count.
Service design / Security & privacy

Designed to protect the information you share.

The planned Directed Ideation MCP is a hosted service, so information sent to it will leave your device. Security is a system of controls and evidence—not a promise that risk is zero.

01 / Access

Authenticated, scoped access

Require secure sign-in and authorize every request against the account that owns the session. Keep customer data isolated between accounts and test those boundaries.

02 / Protection

Limit exposure

Use encrypted connections and encrypted storage, restrict and audit staff access, minimize collection and retention, and keep session content out of diagnostic logs and analytics.

03 / Control

Make data handling visible

Provide clear retention information and authenticated ways to view, export, and delete sessions. Explain deletion timing, including how backups expire.

04 / Evidence

Show what is verifiable

Publish data-flow and security information, independent security-review summaries, and their dates, scope, findings, and limitations. Provide a way to report vulnerabilities.

Planned controls, not a claim of current certification

The initial service is planned as an invite-only US pilot, with US hosting and processing, clear US-only eligibility, and reasonable signup restrictions. Legal review is required before inviting pilot users; access will not be marketed or intentionally offered in other jurisdictions. These restrictions reduce risk but cannot guarantee that foreign laws will not apply. The hosted MCP and these protections are still being developed. Do not submit sensitive or confidential information until the service is available and its current privacy and security disclosures are published. Independent reviews and assurance evidence will be identified by scope and date; no single control or certification eliminates risk.
