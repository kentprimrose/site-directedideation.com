A field guide / from complex situation to tested decision

Better ideas begin before the brainstorm.

Frame the real question. Diagnose root causes and system structure. Generate genuinely different options. Evaluate them against evidence and scenarios. Then decide, stress-test, and learn.

01 / Frame

Define the real question before hunting for answers.

Clarify what kind of situation you face, separate what the problem is from what it is not, and test the assumptions built into your starting point.

01 / Complexity

Match the approach to the situation

Determine whether cause and effect are clear, analyzable with expertise, emergent through small experiments, or urgent and unstable—then let that guide how much analysis or experimentation comes next.

Can we predict the outcome up front, or must we learn by probing?
02 / Boundaries

Separate what is from what is not

Split tangled concerns apart, clarify where and for whom the issue occurs—and where it does not—and set priorities before diving into investigation.

Where does this issue appear, and where does it noticeably not appear?
03 / Assumptions

Question purpose and evidence

Test your framing by distinguishing direct observations from inferences, surfacing hidden assumptions, and examining viewpoints you have not checked yet.

What are we treating as a fact that is actually an assumption?
Capture, then frame

Write observations as needs or tensions—“New team members cannot tell which decision is current,” rather than “We need a new dashboard.” The first leaves room for better solutions.

02 / Diagnose

Find root causes and structure beneath the symptoms.

Look past surface complaints to understand why the pattern persists, which few drivers matter most, and where a focused shift creates real leverage.

A / Root causes

Ask why more than once

Work backward from a specific observed event, marking each causal link as observed or assumed instead of guessing a single cause.

B / Drivers

Measure what moves the gap

Look for the vital few contributors, group causes across distinct categories, and separate routine variation from unusual shifts.

C / Cause chains

Connect shared effects

Map how separate recurring pain points trace back to a common core problem rather than patching each symptom in isolation.

D / Leverage

See the wider system

Spot feedback loops, delays, and incentives that hold the current behavior in place—and the points where a small change shifts outcomes.

Sharpen the direction
Help [specific people] achieve [valuable outcomes] in [context], while respecting [key constraints].
03 / Generate

Produce genuinely different options before judging.

A clear frame and diagnosis make divergent thinking far more useful. Keep idea generation separate from critique and vary how candidate solutions work.

Expand

Defer judgment and vary the mechanism

Turn the working goal into an open “How might we…?” question and generate options that work in distinct ways: remove, automate, substitute, combine, adapt, or reverse a step.

Try: Produce at least six options that rely on different mechanisms.
Analogize

Borrow from unrelated domains

Look outside your immediate field—nature, other industries, or unfamiliar roles—to see how a similar tension is handled elsewhere, then translate the underlying principle back.

Try: Where else is this exact tension solved cleanly?
Combine

Map and recombine dimensions

Break the solution space into independent dimensions—such as trigger, timing, owner, channel, and format—and pair unexpected combinations to reveal overlooked designs.

Try: List four dimensions and force three unfamiliar combinations.
Break patterns

Resolve contradictions and provoke new paths

Name direct tradeoffs explicitly (“improving X worsens Y”) and use deliberate constraints or unlikely stepping-stone provocations to break out of habitual answers.

Try: What could work with no new software and no extra meetings?
04 / Evaluate

Test options against evidence, logic, and scenarios.

Reduce confirmation bias before committing. Check whether reasoning truly holds together, look for disconfirming evidence, and test options across plausible futures.

A / Reasoning

Audit the argument

Check whether observed evidence genuinely supports each recommendation, make hidden links explicit, and test if the supporting structure holds together from the conclusion down.

B / Evidence

Weigh competing explanations

Compare every option against the same body of facts, paying special attention to observations that contradict or weaken a favorite choice.

C / Futures

Test across scenarios

Sketch several plausible future conditions rather than relying on one forecast, and check which options stay useful or adaptable if conditions shift.

D / Perspectives

Separate modes of review

Examine facts, intuition, caution, benefits, and fresh refinements one lens at a time so critique does not silence value and optimism does not hide risk.

05 / Decide & stress-test

Commit clearly, check failure modes, and learn fast.

Make the decision criteria explicit, look proactively for how the chosen approach could fail before acting, and treat the first concrete step as a fast feedback loop.

Compare

Score against explicit criteria

Separate non-negotiable limits from weighted priorities, compare finalists transparently, and identify preventive and fallback actions for top threats.

Try: Which limits are fixed must-haves, and which are weighted tradeoffs?
Uncertainty

Map odds and payoffs

When stakes and uncertainty are high, lay out decision branches, rough likelihoods, and expected outcomes—and check whether a small test reduces the biggest uncertainty.

Try: What single piece of evidence would change our choice?
Stress-test

Imagine failure before starting

Assume the chosen approach has already failed and work backward to surface blind spots; rank concrete failure modes by severity, likelihood, and how early you would detect them.

Try: If this fails in a month, what will have gone wrong first?
Adapt

Run a tight learning cycle

Commit to one smallest concrete first action and observable confirming and challenging evidence. Once results come back, observe, re-orient, decide, and adjust quickly.

Try: What is the smallest step one person can test this week?
A worked example

From a vague complaint to a stress-tested decision.

Imagine a team that keeps losing track of decisions made in meetings. “We need better meetings” is too broad to direct creative or strategic work.

01 / Frame

Define the tension

Clarify the scope: decisions are made in meetings, but people—especially new colleagues—cannot tell which decision is current or who owns it.

02 / Diagnose

Find the root cause

Trace recurring confusion back to scattered chat threads and unowned notes, with no single place updated when a choice changes.

03 / Generate

Explore varied options

Produce six distinct mechanisms: a one-paragraph project-page log, a weekly digest, a decision-owner sign-off, an automated summary, a shared tag, and retiring side threads.

04 / Evaluate

Test against evidence

Compare options for maintenance burden, onboarding clarity, and resilience during busy release weeks; notice that heavy tooling fails under time pressure.

05 / Decide & stress-test

Run one small test

Add a one-paragraph decision record to the existing project page, pre-check the risk of forgotten updates, and ask three colleagues to locate a decision a week later.

Put it to work / 20 minutes

Take one real challenge through all five steps.

Use a notebook, work with a partner, or run a guided MCP session. Keep framing, diagnosis, options, evaluation, and commitment distinct until you have explored each one.

  1. Frame: What concrete friction or decision are you facing, what is inside versus outside scope, and how predictable is the situation?
  2. Diagnose: What root causes, system patterns, or assumptions explain why this keeps happening?
  3. Generate: Tied to a clear working goal, what are at least six genuinely different approaches?
  4. Evaluate: What evidence supports or challenges the top options, and how do they hold up under different scenarios?
  5. Decide & stress-test: Which option wins on clear criteria, how could it fail, and what smallest first action and observable evidence will test it?
MCP companion tools

Keep the core process distinct from unblocking tools.

Directed ideation is the five-step process for framing, diagnosing, generating, evaluating, and deciding. When you hit a blockage during an MCP-guided session, additional standalone tools help you get unstuck without changing the core process.

Core process

Directed Ideation

Five revisable phases—frame, diagnose, generate, evaluate, and decide and stress-test—supported by phase-level guidance, interview prompts, diagnostic checks, and technique menus.

Purpose: Orient the work, clarify tradeoffs, and guide ideas toward a stress-tested decision.
Additional tool

Lateral Thinking

Not a phase of directed ideation, but a separate optional MCP tool to assist you past creative or framing blockages by questioning assumptions, shifting perspective, and exploring fresh options before judging them.

Purpose: Break through stuck thinking, then carry any useful principle back to your direction.
Additional tool

Requirements Clarification

Not a phase of directed ideation, but a separate optional MCP tool to untangle vague complaints, separate symptoms from underlying causes, clarify boundaries, and turn broad wants into observable criteria.

Purpose: Replace ambiguity with clear causes and measurable criteria, then return to the active phase.
Additional tool

Strategic Thinking

Not a phase of directed ideation, but a separate optional MCP tool to examine system-wide causes, surface hidden assumptions behind tradeoffs, evaluate options from multiple angles, and work through execution obstacles.

Purpose: Resolve persistent conflicts, unintended side effects, and execution obstacles, then return to your direction.
How they fit together

Directed ideation sets the compass; additional MCP tools help when progress stalls. Use them when ideas feel too narrow, requirements feel vague, or conflicts and obstacles block movement—then test every insight against your goal, constraints, and evidence. Service design / Security & privacy

Keep session context with your calling agent.

The Directed Ideation MCP provides five-phase method guidance and additional support tools (for lateral thinking, requirements clarification, and strategic thinking) without receiving or storing your challenge, conversation, goals, or ideas. The calling agent manages that context and its own provider’s policies determine how it is handled.

01 / Access

Authenticated, scoped access

Uses secure sign-in and authorizes account and team operations against the authenticated user, isolating account and team records across tested boundaries.

02 / Protection

Limit exposure

Uses encrypted connections and encrypted storage for the limited account and team data the service requires, restricts and audits staff access, and keeps session content out of service requests, logs, and analytics.

03 / Context boundary

Session data stays with the agent

The service does not store session history, phase progress, or session content. It cannot control the calling agent’s storage, model use, retention, or sharing; check that provider’s privacy controls.

04 / Evidence

Show what is verifiable

Publishes data-flow and security information, independent review summaries with their dates, scope, findings, and limitations, and a channel to report vulnerabilities.

Scope and security boundary

The hosted MCP operates as an invite-only US service with US-based hosting, clear US eligibility, and signup restrictions; access is not marketed or intentionally offered in other jurisdictions, though restrictions cannot guarantee that foreign laws will not apply. Session context is managed solely by your calling agent and its provider, not this MCP service, so this boundary does not guarantee end-to-end privacy. Keep confidential or regulated data within your agent provider’s approved controls. Published security reviews and assurance summaries identify their specific scope and date; no single control or certification eliminates risk.
