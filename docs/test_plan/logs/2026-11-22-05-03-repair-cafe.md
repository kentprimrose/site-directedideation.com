## Final plans

### MCP

**Try handing out queue numbers at the door before routing, for the first 20 minutes.** The floater gives each arrival a number, explains aloud that it holds their place but doesn’t send them to a bench, and makes sure they can wait seated without losing their spot. The greeter still provides spoken routing help, and electrical items still get assessed before going to a bench. At minute 20, the floater returns to tools and supplies; the greeter resumes handing out numbers at intake and honors the numbers already issued.

**Why this beats the strongest alternative:** Having the floater handle routine routing could reduce the greeter’s workload more directly, but you don’t yet know whether the floater can do it confidently. Splitting off ticketing tests whether a clear place in line helps people stay, without asking the floater to make routing decisions. The risk is that it just moves the wait to after ticketing.

**What to measure:** Use one tally sheet to count all arrivals, including people who never take a number. Count departures before a bench, split into before and after ticketing. The main target is **no more than 6 of roughly 45 arrivals leaving before reaching a bench**, compared with 11 of 44 last time. For visitors who need routing help, note arrival time and when that help starts, including around the minute-20 handoff. Compare with a comparable event if you have timing data; without it, report those waits but don’t claim they didn’t increase. Verify that no required item checks were skipped.

**What would make us revise it:** Stop the trial if seated visitors lose their place, people needing routing help get held back, or an item bypasses a required check. If pre-ticket departures fall but total departures miss the target at a comparable event, don’t keep the change just because the ticket line looks better. Look at where people now wait, including after the floater leaves.

What remains uncertain is why the 11 visitors left, whether their items were repairable, and whether the extra door coverage will shorten the wait or simply make it easier to tolerate.

### Control

**Try a two-person intake for the first 20 minutes.** Give the floater a simple bench map so they can ticket and route obvious non-electrical items. The greeter handles electrical and unclear items, and unassessed electrical items still don’t go straight to a bench. Before opening, agree on who calls the next visitor for an idle bench. At minute 20, the floater hands back intake and returns to tools and supplies.

**Why this first:** The clearest bottleneck is before ticketing: visitors are already waiting while some benches are idle. The strongest alternative—giving out numbers immediately and routing everyone afterward—would make the queue visible, but it could just move the same wait to after ticketing. Splitting routine routing from cases that need the greeter gives idle benches a chance to start sooner. It won’t add electrical repair capacity.

**Measure arrival to bench, not ticket to bench.** Keep the anonymous door tally and record arrival, ticket, and bench-start times where possible. Count departures before and after ticketing, and mark unknown outcomes separately. Report the first 20 minutes separately, watch for a queue rebuilding in minutes 20–25, and compare the whole-event result with the last event’s **11 of 44 observed departures before a bench**, including **five before ticketing**. The posted 18-minute median excluded everyone who left, so don’t use it as the success benchmark.

**What would make us revise it:** If tickets come faster but bench starts don’t, and benches are still idle, fix who calls the next visitor rather than adding more door help. If non-electrical visitors reach benches sooner but the remaining waits and departures are concentrated in electrical, test clearer electrical wait estimates next. If the line mainly rebuilds when the floater leaves, adjust the handoff before abandoning the split.

The main uncertainties are who currently calls visitors, how many departures the tally misses, and whether the next crowd brings a similar mix of items. Treat one event as a useful signal, not proof.
