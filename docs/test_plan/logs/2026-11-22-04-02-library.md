## Final plans

### MCP

**Try a cancellation-triggered waitlist check for four weeks.** Whenever staff log a same-day cancellation on a weekday, have the person who logs it check the paper waitlist and call patrons who opted in for short-notice openings, in waitlist order. Stop when the slot is booked or the desk reaches its ten-minute daily cap. Keep appointment hours, existing booking options, and walk-in access unchanged. Don’t hold slots for callbacks or leave appointment details in voicemail.

**Why this beats a daily openings check for this pilot:** It adds one step to a process staff already follow and fixes a gap you’ve observed: none of the three logged cancellations prompted a waitlist check. A daily check could also fill never-booked slots, so it has more upside, but you don’t yet know whether the desk can do it reliably in ten minutes. The tradeoff is real: if last month’s pattern repeats, refilling all three cancellations would bring unused slots from 16 to 13, one short of the target.

**Measure:** Compare unused slots with the prior four weeks: **12 or fewer is the target**. Use the planned categories—unbooked, missed, cancelled, or refilled—and count a refill only when the replacement patron completes the session. For each weekday cancellation, note whether staff checked the list and offered the slot. Track desk minutes, privacy complaints, and any instance where the routine delays or blocks ordinary in-person booking or walk-in help. Success requires no privacy complaints and no reduction in access for people without a phone.

**Revise based on what happens.** If staff miss eligible cancellations, fix who owns the check. If a privacy or access problem surfaces, pause and correct it. If staff reliably refill openings but there are too few cancellations to reach 12, try a time-boxed check for still-unbooked slots next rather than asking staff to make more calls. What remains uncertain is how many cancellations will occur, how many patrons can come on short notice, and how much of any month-to-month change is due to the pilot.

### Control

**Try one month of brief, opt-in reminder calls.** Keep appointment hours and walk-in access unchanged. Within the desk’s existing ten weekday minutes, make one call attempt the day before each appointment when staff are available. If you reach the patron, confirm the branch, date, and time. If they’ve separately opted into voicemail, leave only a neutral callback message—no appointment details or description of what they’re getting help with. Otherwise, leave no message. Don’t add weekend coverage for the pilot; log appointments you couldn’t call.

**Why this over calling the waitlist:** Five of the six missed appointments involved a wrong branch, a wrong date, or forgetting—the problems a reminder can address. The waitlist is the strongest alternative, but it could have recovered at most the three recorded same-day cancellations, and nobody knows whether those openings came with enough notice. The earlier reminder trial showed patrons would confirm or correct details, not that calls improved attendance. This pilot tests that.

**What to measure:** Use a paper tally for call eligibility, attempts, live contacts, corrections, and desk minutes. For each slot, record whether it was used, never booked, missed, or cancelled and left empty. Count a slot as used if someone attends it, even if it isn’t the original patron. Compare with the previous four weeks: **16 of 48 unused, including six missed**.

**When to revise:** If you offer 48 slots again, look for **four or fewer misses and 14 or fewer unused slots**, without exceeding the daily time cap or compromising privacy. Treat that as a practical signal, not proof. If calls don’t reach many patrons, routinely hit the cap, or don’t reduce misses, try the opt-in waitlist next and record how much notice each opening gives. If a voicemail discloses private details, pause voicemail immediately.

The biggest unknown is how many patrons staff can actually reach within ten minutes. Reminders also won’t directly fix the near-closing slots that never get booked.
