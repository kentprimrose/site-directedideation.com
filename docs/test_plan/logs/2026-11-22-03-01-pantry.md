## Final plans

### MCP

**Try a one-page verification checklist at the existing check-in station next Saturday.** Keep one line, one roster, and the same required checks. Don’t open the side table for this trial.

**Set it up in 15 minutes.** Have the checker write the approved steps for the three cases you observed: name mismatch, address change, and first visit. For each, list what to ask, where to look, and what to do if the case is still unresolved. Make clear which steps are required. Keep the sheet beside the roster and handle exceptions in place, without sending anyone to the back of the line.

**Why it beats the side table for this trial:** A staffed side table could keep routine check-ins moving during a long case. But it depends on the floater being free and on getting the household promptly back to the checker. That’s least reliable during the 9:20–9:40 restocking peak. The checklist works through that stretch and tests whether avoidable lookup or discussion accounts for enough of the delay to matter.

**Measure the full trip, not just check-in.** On anonymous first-hour slips, record arrival, check-in start and finish, and pickup time. Mark each slip *routine* or *extra verification*, with no personal details. Sample across the hour, including the restocking peak, and include both groups. Report the longest sampled arrival-to-pickup time overall and for each group. Also log arrivals, truck delays, restocking interruptions, any sustained handoff line, and total households served. Compare with a normal Saturday at similar demand where you have records.

**Keep or revise it based on what you see.** Repeat the trial if the observed maximum is **20 minutes or less**, with all checks preserved, no sign of material added delay for households needing extra verification, no reduction in households served at comparable demand, and no new handoff line. If waits still exceed 20 minutes and the checker is losing time to lookup or repeated questions, revise the checklist around that specific step. If the time is mostly required verification, don’t trim the checks: reconsider a staffed-only side table when you can guarantee a prompt return to the checker. If the checklist causes a missed check or confusion about what’s required, stop using it and return to the existing process.

The main uncertainty is how much of those 3–5 minutes the checklist can save. A small sample gives you an **observed** maximum, not proof that nobody waited longer; without comparable baseline timings, it also can’t establish how much waits improved.

### Control

**Next Saturday, try a shorter, standardized way to handle exceptions at the existing check-in station.** Keep the same volunteer and roster, and finish each household’s check-in in order. The goal is to cut repeated lookup and discussion, not verification.

Use the 15-minute setup to have the check-in volunteer write a short prompt for each common case: name mismatch, address change, and first visit. For each, note the first question they already use to clarify the issue and the next step under the current process. At check-in, ask only for what’s needed, keep identifying details out of earshot, and use the full existing process whenever the quick clarification doesn’t resolve the issue. Don’t add paperwork for households, change food choice, or move anyone out of line.

**Why this beats the strongest alternative:** A private side table could do more to keep the line moving *if* someone could reliably staff it and handle part of the work, or if the roster volunteer could pause a case without costing the household its place. Neither is established, and the floater already had to leave a second station to restock. The single-station trial is less ambitious, but it targets the five slow check-ins that blocked the line without depending on extra coverage or an unconfirmed rule change.

**What to measure:** In the first hour, repeat the 20-check-in timing sample if you can do it without pulling someone off a station. Mark each check-in as routine or exception, with no names or addresses. Also spot-check arrival-to-pickup waits during the opening rush and note any stock or handoff disruption. Look for fewer exceptions in the **3–5 minute range**, routine check-ins still near **45 seconds**, and shorter pickup waits. The **32- and 34-minute** longest waits are useful context, but a sampled maximum isn’t a like-for-like comparison.

**When to revise:** Stop the trial if it compromises privacy, verification, or service. If exceptions still take 3–5 minutes on a normal-stock Saturday, find out whether the time is going to required verification or avoidable lookup before investing in a side table. If exceptions get faster but pickup waits don’t, look next at the opening surge: 30 arrivals in 15 minutes would take about 22½ minutes to check in even if every case were routine. The main uncertainty is how much of the exception time is actually avoidable; this trial tests that without shifting the burden onto households.
